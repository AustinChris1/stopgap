When a drug goes into shortage, a hospital pharmacist rebuilds the supply picture by hand: the FDA shortage file, each manufacturer's resupply note, the formulary. Vizient measured that at roughly 20 million hours across US hospitals in 2023, and Bluesight found 69.7% still doing it on spreadsheets.

Stopgap drafts that packet instead, and refuses to finish it.

Given one package NDC in shortage, it reads openFDA, RxNorm and DailyMed live at request time and returns a sourced supply packet: the pinned row, other packages at the same concentration, other presentations, and anything excluded by a decision the pharmacy already made. Every row carries its labeler, package NDC, availability, the date the FDA row changed, the manufacturer's own resupply note, its RxNorm concept and a link to its FDA label. A judge can click any citation and land on the source.

The rule that makes it safe is the grouping. Rows are split by the concentration printed on each individual row, so a 40 mg/mL concentrate vial never merges with a premixed dextrose bag at 0.8 mg/mL. There is no ingredient-level join anywhere in the code, because an ingredient join merges exactly those two things. On 27 October the file returned eleven dopamine packages: four concentrate vials, seven bags, and of the four only one fully available while two came from the same labeler already short.

What it will not do is the point. It never names, ranks or recommends a substitute. Same ingredient is not the same product, and route, indication, dosing and contraindications are checked by nothing here. The clinical conclusion field is empty by construction and the signature and date lines are blank. The openFDA "do not rely on this for medical care" sentence and the NLM attribution print on the packet itself.

This is not the first shortage tool. The wedge is narrower: a cited draft that deliberately stops where clinical judgement starts.
